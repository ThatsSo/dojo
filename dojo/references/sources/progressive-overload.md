# Progressive overload

> Status: written by Claude (2026-11-07), pending James's write-up. Everything here is *(Claude)* until then.

## Core idea
A body adapts to the demands placed on it, then stops adapting once those demands become routine. To keep improving, the load has to rise a little at a time: enough to require adaptation, not so much that it causes injury or failure. Adaptation happens during recovery, not during the effort itself, so planned lighter periods are part of the method, not a break from it. The term comes from strength training: the physician Thomas DeLorme's progressive-resistance exercise in the 1940s, and the long-told story of Milo of Croton carrying a growing calf.

## Key techniques
- **Small increments:** raise one variable at a time by a small amount. In lifting, that's about 2.5–5% more weight, one more rep, or one more set. Big jumps cause failed sets and bad form.
- **Choose one variable to overload:** load (weight), volume (sets × reps), density (less rest), range or complexity. Change one, and hold the rest steady.
- **Progress when the current level holds:** a common rule is "add weight when you complete all sets and reps with good form" (double progression: first add reps up to a target, then add weight and drop back to the bottom of the rep range).
- **Track everything:** a training log makes each increase a decision rather than a feeling.
- **Deload:** every few weeks, or when performance drops or fatigue builds, train lighter (often about 40–60% of normal volume or intensity) for a week, then resume. Many lifters come back stronger.
- **Periodization:** plan cycles of building and recovery rather than straight-line increases forever.

## Examples
- *Lifting:* squat 60 kg for 3×5. When all three sets succeed with good form, go to 62.5 kg next session. After about 6 weeks of gains, deload to about 70% for a week.
- *Running:* the "10% rule": raise weekly distance by no more than about 10% a week (a guideline, not a law).
- *Learning (by analogy):* raise tempo by 4–5 bpm once a passage is clean; add one new variable to a drill once the current version holds; Voltaic's rank tiers; Woodpecker's time limits halving only while accuracy holds.

## Caveats
- **Evidence:** established for strength and endurance adaptation in exercise science. Specific numbers (2.5–5%, 10% a week, deload timing) are practitioner guidelines that vary by person and sport.
- **As a model for learning:** practitioner-level analogy. Cognitive skills don't fatigue like muscles, but "small steps, one variable, track it, plan easier sessions" matches the goldilocks-zone and spacing evidence.
- Overload without recovery leads to overtraining. In learning, the equivalent is burnout and quitting (A4).
