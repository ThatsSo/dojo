## Part 1: The Learning Method

### 1. Establish a Baseline — Measure Current Ability

- Progressive Overload: Gradually increase the demands placed on a system as it adapts, forcing continued development.
- In physical training, overload stimulates adaptations in strength, endurance, or muscle. In learning, appropriately increased demands can develop knowledge, speed, accuracy, complexity, and independence.
- Identify the target skill and determine current performance.
- Measure relevant dimensions:
  - Accuracy: How often is performance correct?
  - Speed: How quickly can tasks be completed?
  - Complexity: How difficult are the tasks currently manageable?
  - Independence: How much assistance is required?
  - Consistency: How reliably can performance be repeated?
- Rule: Establish what the learner can currently accomplish before deciding how to increase difficulty.

### 2. Apply Progressive Overload — Increase Learning Demands

- Introduce challenges that exceed current comfortable performance without making successful learning impossible.
- Increase difficulty through one or more Overload Variables:
  1. Intensity: Increase the difficulty of individual problems or tasks.
  2. Volume: Increase the number of problems, repetitions, or total practice performed.
  3. Density: Complete the same amount of work in less time or with shorter breaks.
  4. Complexity: Introduce additional steps, interacting concepts, or variables.
  5. Precision: Require greater accuracy, detail, or quality.
  6. Independence: Reduce hints, instructions, worked examples, or external assistance.
  7. Variability: Introduce unfamiliar examples, contexts, or task conditions.
  8. Retention: Increase the delay before information must be recalled or applied.
- Rule: Increase meaningful task demands gradually, selecting variables that develop the desired capability.

### 3. Maintain the Optimal Challenge Zone — Avoid Underload and Overload

- Underload: Tasks are consistently easy and require little attention or adaptation.
- Productive Overload: Tasks require genuine effort while remaining achievable with current knowledge or manageable assistance.
- Excessive Overload: Tasks exceed available abilities, causing confusion, repeated failure, or reliance on shortcuts.
- When underloaded, increase an appropriate difficulty variable.
- When excessively overloaded, reduce demands or practice missing prerequisites.
- Rule: Train near the edge of current competence, not so far beyond it that effective practice breaks down.

### 4. Establish Progression Criteria — Earn Higher Difficulty

- Define objective standards indicating readiness to advance.
- Possible progression requirements:
  - Repeatedly meeting an accuracy threshold.
  - Completing tasks within a target time.
  - Demonstrating understanding without hints.
  - Producing consistent results across multiple sessions.
  - Successfully applying the skill to unfamiliar examples.
- Avoid advancing because of one unusually successful attempt.
- Rule: Increase difficulty when performance becomes reliably competent, not simply after a predetermined amount of time.

### 5. Apply Small Increments — Progress Without Destabilizing Technique

- Increase task demands in manageable steps rather than making abrupt jumps.
- Microloading: Introduce very small increases when larger increases would cause excessive difficulty.
- Examples:
  - Piano: Increase tempo by 3–5 BPM.
  - Mathematics: Add one additional step or constraint.
  - Languages: Introduce one unfamiliar grammatical construction.
  - Programming: Remove one layer of guidance from a familiar problem.
- Rule: Use the smallest meaningful increase that creates additional challenge while preserving correct execution.

### 6. Repeat, Adapt, Progress — Use a Continuous Feedback Loop

- Follow the Progressive Overload Cycle:
  1. Assess: Measure present performance.
  2. Select: Choose a task near current ability.
  3. Overload: Increase one relevant demand.
  4. Practice: Perform repeated, deliberate attempts.
  5. Recover: Allow adequate rest and consolidation.
  6. Evaluate: Measure accuracy, speed, independence, and consistency.
  7. Adjust: Increase, maintain, or reduce difficulty based on results.
- Rule: Treat difficulty as a continuously adjustable variable rather than a fixed property of the learning material.

## Part 2: Underlying Learning Principles

### 1. Specific Adaptation — Train What You Want to Improve

- Specificity Principle: Adaptations tend to reflect the demands repeatedly practiced.
- Increasing speed does not necessarily improve conceptual depth.
- Increasing problem complexity does not automatically improve rapid recall.
- Match overload variables to the target capability:
  - Speed: Reduce available time.
  - Accuracy: Increase precision requirements.
  - Understanding: Require explanations, derivations, or justifications.
  - Flexibility: Vary task conditions.
  - Independence: Remove assistance.
  - Retention: Increase retrieval intervals.
- Rule: Overload the particular ability that requires improvement rather than increasing difficulty indiscriminately.

### 2. Adaptation and Diminishing Challenge — Yesterday's Difficulty Becomes Today's Routine

- Repeated exposure can make once-difficult tasks increasingly manageable.
- As competence improves, an unchanged task may provide less developmental challenge.
- Adaptation: The learner becomes more capable of handling previously demanding conditions.
- Rule: Reassess task difficulty as skills develop and increase demands when the current workload no longer provides sufficient challenge.

### 3. Cognitive Load — Respect Processing Limits

- Cognitive Load: The demands placed on limited working-memory resources.
- Distinguish:
  - Intrinsic Load: Complexity inherent to the task.
  - Extraneous Load: Unnecessary complexity caused by presentation or poor instruction.
- Productive overload increases meaningful task demands without introducing avoidable confusion.
- Reduce irrelevant distractions, unclear instructions, and unnecessary steps.
- Rule: Increase relevant difficulty while minimizing unnecessary cognitive load.

### 4. Scaffolding and Independence — Progressively Remove Assistance

- Scaffolding: Temporary support that enables learners to complete tasks beyond their independent ability.
- Supports may include examples, hints, templates, explanations, or guided demonstrations.
- Fading: Gradually reduce assistance as competence increases.
- Example progression:
  1. Follow a worked example.
  2. Complete a partially worked example.
  3. Solve independently with hints available.
  4. Solve independently without hints.
  5. Solve unfamiliar variations without guidance.
- Rule: Treat reduced dependence on assistance as a legitimate form of progressive overload.

### 5. Retrieval Strength — Increase the Demands on Memory

- Frequently reviewing information immediately can make recall easy without ensuring durable retention.
- Increasing retrieval intervals challenges long-term memory.
- Spacing: Extend the delay between successful reviews.
- Desirable Difficulty: Appropriately effortful retrieval can improve retention.
- Rule: Gradually increase retrieval difficulty as memories stabilize, while avoiding intervals that produce excessive forgetting.

### 6. Automaticity — Progress From Accuracy to Fluency

- Automaticity: Performing familiar operations rapidly with little conscious effort.
- Early practice may emphasize slow, accurate execution.
- Later practice can increase speed requirements while maintaining accuracy.
- Example progression:
  1. Perform correctly without time pressure.
  2. Repeat until accuracy stabilizes.
  3. Introduce moderate time constraints.
  4. Reduce completion time progressively.
  5. Apply the skill under realistic conditions.
- Rule: Build accurate execution before aggressively overloading speed.

### 7. Recovery and Consolidation — Adapt Between Sessions

- Learning does not occur exclusively during active practice.
- Consolidation: Memory and skill representations can stabilize and reorganize after training, including during sleep.
- Excessive practice can cause fatigue, reduced attention, and declining performance.
- Unlike physical training, different cognitive skills have different recovery requirements; there is no universal rest interval.
- Rule: Balance demanding practice with adequate sleep, breaks, and opportunities for consolidation.

### 8. Individualized Progression — Adjust to the Learner

- Learners differ in prior knowledge, processing speed, fatigue tolerance, and rates of improvement.
- The same challenge may be productive for one learner and overwhelming for another.
- Progression rates should respond to actual performance.
- Rule: Use individual results to guide difficulty rather than imposing identical increases on everyone.

## Part 3: Programming Learning Like Exercise

*Dojo application: how this source is applied to learning design, not the source's own claim.*

### 1. Repetitions — Count Meaningful Attempts

- Repetition: One complete attempt at a problem, retrieval, movement, or task.
- Repetitions should require active performance rather than passive exposure.
- Measure successful and unsuccessful attempts.
- Rule: Count deliberate attempts that exercise the target skill, not merely time spent studying.

### 2. Sets — Organize Practice Into Focused Blocks

- Set: A group of related repetitions performed together.
- Example: Three sets of five increasingly challenging accounting problems.
- Use sets to manage concentration and evaluate performance.
- Rule: Group repetitions into manageable blocks with clear objectives and feedback.

### 3. Intensity — Adjust Task Difficulty

- Intensity: The difficulty of each attempt relative to current competence.
- Higher intensity may involve harder problems, stricter precision, or greater independence.
- High-intensity practice generally requires more deliberate attention.
- Rule: Increase intensity when existing tasks become reliably manageable.

### 4. Volume — Adjust Total Practice

- Volume: The total quantity of meaningful practice performed.
- Increase volume to gain additional exposure, repetition, or reinforcement.
- More volume is not always beneficial if attention or accuracy deteriorates.
- Rule: Add practice volume only while repetitions remain sufficiently productive.

### 5. Density — Increase Work per Unit of Time

- Density: The amount of practice completed within a given duration.
- Increase density by reducing breaks or completing familiar tasks more efficiently.
- Density training is useful when speed or sustained performance matters.
- Rule: Increase density without allowing rushing to undermine the target skill.

### 6. Progressive Complexity — Add Interacting Demands

- Increase the number of concepts or operations that must be coordinated.
- Example:
  1. Solve a single-step problem.
  2. Solve a multi-step problem.
  3. Combine multiple concepts.
  4. Solve an unfamiliar integrated problem.
  5. Apply the solution in a realistic environment.
- Rule: Build isolated components before overloading their coordination.

### 7. Deloading — Temporarily Reduce Difficulty or Volume

- Deload: A planned or reactive reduction in training demands.
- Consider reducing workload when performance consistently deteriorates, fatigue accumulates, or attention becomes unreliable.
- Options:
  - Reduce task difficulty.
  - Reduce practice volume.
  - Increase rest.
  - Revisit fundamentals.
- Rule: Reduce demands temporarily when recovery or technical correction is more valuable than further overload.

### 8. Periodization — Vary Training Emphasis Over Time

- Periodization: Organize training into phases emphasizing different capabilities.
- Possible learning phases:
  1. Foundation: Build understanding and accurate technique.
  2. Volume: Accumulate varied practice and strengthen recognition.
  3. Intensity: Increase difficulty and independent problem-solving.
  4. Performance: Practice under realistic time and environmental demands.
  5. Assessment and Recovery: Measure progress, consolidate learning, and adjust the next phase.
- Rule: Vary the emphasis of practice according to current development needs rather than maximizing every training variable simultaneously.

## Part 4: Practical Applications

*Dojo application: how this source is applied to learning design, not the source's own claim.*

| Skill            | Baseline Task                               | Progressive Overload                                                                                     |
| ---------------- | ------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Piano            | Play a short passage slowly and accurately  | Increase tempo, rhythmic complexity, hand independence, or sight-reading difficulty                      |
| Chess            | Solve familiar tactical patterns            | Increase puzzle difficulty, reduce solution time, or combine unfamiliar tactical motifs                  |
| Accounting       | Classify straightforward transactions       | Add ambiguity, multi-step adjustments, unfamiliar cases, or independent analysis                         |
| Spanish          | Construct simple sentences with guidance    | Increase sentence complexity, speaking speed, unfamiliar vocabulary, or spontaneous conversation         |
| FPS Aim Training | Track predictable, moderately sized targets | Increase movement speed, reduce target size, introduce unpredictability, or combine aiming with movement |
| Programming      | Complete a guided implementation            | Remove hints, add constraints, increase system complexity, or require independent debugging              |

## Part 5: Common Pitfalls and Corrections

*Merged into `pitfalls.md`; kept here as this source's own list.*

| Pitfall                       | Cause                                                                     | Corrective Rule                                       |
| ----------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------- |
| Permanent Underload           | Repeating tasks that have become effortless                               | Increase meaningful difficulty as competence improves |
| Excessive Overload            | Advancing beyond current abilities                                        | Reduce difficulty or strengthen prerequisites         |
| Progressing Too Quickly       | Increasing demands before skills stabilize                                | Require consistent performance before advancement     |
| Increasing Everything at Once | Simultaneously raising speed, complexity, and volume                      | Adjust one primary variable at a time                 |
| Overemphasis on Speed         | Prioritizing fast answers over correct reasoning                          | Establish accuracy and understanding first            |
| Junk Volume                   | Adding repetitions without attention or feedback                          | Prioritize deliberate, meaningful attempts            |
| Unnecessary Complexity        | Poor instructions or distracting task design                              | Minimize extraneous cognitive load                    |
| Ignoring Recovery             | Repeated high-intensity sessions despite fatigue                          | Include breaks, sleep, and deloads when appropriate   |
| Misaligned Overload           | Training difficulty unrelated to the intended skill                       | Apply the specificity principle                       |
| Assistance Dependence         | Completing tasks only with extensive guidance                             | Progressively fade scaffolding                        |
| False Progress                | Improving on familiar exercises without transfer                          | Assess unfamiliar problems and real-world tasks       |
| Rigid Progression             | Increasing difficulty according to a fixed schedule regardless of results | Use performance-based progression                     |

## Core Operating Rules (this source's summary)

*Merged into `rules.md`, which is the binding list; kept here as this source's own summary.*

- Establish a Baseline: Measure accuracy, speed, complexity, independence, and consistency.
- Define the Target Adaptation: Identify the specific capability that needs improvement.
- Apply Specificity: Increase demands directly related to the desired skill.
- Use Productive Overload: Train slightly beyond comfortable ability without overwhelming the learner.
- Adjust Overload Variables: Manipulate intensity, volume, density, complexity, precision, independence, variability, and retention.
- Progress in Small Increments: Introduce the smallest meaningful increase in difficulty.
- Earn Progression: Advance after demonstrating consistent competence rather than one successful attempt.
- Prioritize Accuracy Before Speed: Establish correct execution before imposing aggressive time constraints.
- Increase Independence: Gradually remove hints, examples, and assistance.
- Manage Cognitive Load: Increase relevant complexity while minimizing unnecessary difficulty.
- Track Meaningful Repetitions: Measure actual attempts and performance rather than study time alone.
- Balance Intensity and Volume: Adjust difficulty and quantity without sacrificing practice quality.
- Use Progressive Complexity: Combine previously mastered components into increasingly demanding tasks.
- Extend Retrieval Intervals: Increase memory demands as retention stabilizes.
- Monitor Adaptation: Reassess tasks as previously difficult work becomes manageable.
- Include Recovery: Allow adequate breaks, sleep, and consolidation.
- Deload When Necessary: Temporarily reduce demands during excessive fatigue or deteriorating performance.
- Use Periodization: Alternate phases emphasizing foundation, volume, intensity, performance, and assessment.
- Individualize Progression: Adjust difficulty based on actual learning outcomes.
- Validate Transfer: Confirm that improvements generalize beyond practiced examples.

Central principle: Learning demands must increase as competence develops. Just as unchanged exercise loads eventually provide less stimulus for physical adaptation, unchanged learning tasks may become too easy to drive further growth. The goal is not perpetual difficulty, but a continuous cycle of challenge → practice → feedback → adaptation → increased challenge, with progression guided by demonstrated performance rather than effort alone.
