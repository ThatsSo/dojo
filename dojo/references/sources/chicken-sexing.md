# Chicken sexing (implicit perceptual learning)

> Status: written by Claude (2026-11-07), building on James's essay (the "Implicit vs Explicit learning" section) and pending his write-up. Text marked *(Claude)* is not from James.

## Core idea
Newly hatched chicks look almost identical, yet trained "chick sexers" can sort them by sex at high speed and high accuracy. James's point: many experts can't put into words how they do it. The skill was passed on by doing: the novice guesses, the master confirms or corrects, thousands of times, until the right judgement becomes perception rather than reasoning. It's the clearest example of knowledge that lives "in the eyes and hands" rather than in sentences, and of repetition refining intuition. *(Claude)* The famous research twist is that it's not purely tacit: Biederman & Shiffrar (1987) asked an expert which cue mattered most, gave novices a short explicit description of it with a diagram, and the novices' accuracy jumped from about chance to close to the experts'.

## Key techniques
- **High-volume classification with instant feedback:** the learner makes a call on each item, and an expert immediately says right or wrong. Volume plus fast, accurate feedback is the engine.
- **Real examples, labelled by an expert:** practise on the actual thing, not a description of it.
- **A few explicit cues, not a rulebook** *(Claude)*: a single well-chosen pointer ("look at this shape here") can shortcut early learning. A long list of rules can't replace volume.
- **Relax into it:** James notes that overthinking hurts. The judgement has to become perception, which links to the attitude side (A5).
- **Speed comes after accuracy:** professional sexers were trained toward both, but correctness was confirmed first.

## Examples
- The traditional training (historically associated with Japanese vent-sexing schools): apprentices sort chicks under a master's eye for months, with every call confirmed or corrected.
- *(Claude)* Biederman & Shiffrar (1987): a one-page explicit instruction about one key cue took novices from near chance to near expert accuracy on a picture-based test.
- *(Claude)* Similar "perceptual expertise" shows up in radiologists reading X-rays, birders identifying species from a glimpse, and chess players seeing patterns (chunking, see `woodpecker.md`).

## Fit with James's synthesis
It's James's main evidence that "knowledge can be felt even if it can't be reduced to words", and that "the path to mastery is not thinking harder, but practicing longer". The research twist supports his own conclusion that "sometimes you do need both explicit and implicit knowledge": a few explicit cues to aim the eyes, then volume and feedback to build the perception. It touches T7 (explicit vs. implicit, resolved by how fast decisions must be made).

## Principles supported
- **S14:** explicit vs. implicit. The core example: perceptual skill from volume plus feedback, sped up by a few cues.
- **S6:** fast, informative feedback on every attempt.
- **S12:** when the patterns are the skill, repeated exposure to the same kinds of items builds recognition.
- **S4:** making a call before seeing the answer (retrieval-like).
- **A5:** relaxed attention; overthinking blocks perception.
- **Design implication:** for perceptual skills (ear training, reading notation, identifying chords, spotting audit risks), build rapid "which is it?" drills with instant correction and expert-labelled examples, plus a short explicit cue for each distinction.

## Caveats
- **Evidence:** the practice is well documented; Biederman & Shiffrar (1987) is a well-known but small study using photographs, not live chicks. Treat the size of the jump as indicative, not exact.
- **The popular story overstates it:** "no explicit knowledge exists" is false. Experts can often name some cues when asked carefully. Both layers matter.
- **Labels must be right:** feedback is only as good as the expert giving it, and learning from wrong labels trains wrong perception.
