## Part 1: The Learning Method

### 1. Skill Decomposition — Divide Complex Abilities Into Sub-Skills

- Treat complex performance as a collection of distinct, trainable skills rather than one general ability.
- Divide the target skill into core categories, then into subcategories that isolate particular techniques.
- FPS Aim Training Example:
  - Clicking: Rapidly acquiring targets and executing accurate shots.
    - Static: Precise movements toward stationary targets.
    - Dynamic: Timing and accuracy against moving targets.
    - Linear: Hybrid of static and dynamic techniques.
  - Tracking: Continuously following a moving target.
    - Precise: Smooth control against small or predictable movements.
    - Reactive: Responding to unpredictable movements without overpredicting.
    - Control: Hybrid of precision and reactivity.
  - Switching: Rapidly transitioning between targets while maintaining accuracy.
    - Speed: Fast transitions between easy targets.
    - Evasive: Accurate transitions into difficult moving targets.
    - Stability: Hybrid of speed and control.
- Recognize that different sub-skills may require different or opposing techniques.
- Rule: Identify and train distinct components independently rather than assuming proficiency in one transfers automatically to others.

### 2. Isolated Practice — Make Weaknesses Measurable

- Design exercises that emphasize one specific skill component.
- Reduce unrelated variables that could obscure the skill being evaluated.
- Minimize unnecessary randomness to improve consistency.
- Examples:
  - Reaction Training: Use unpredictable changes that require genuine responses.
  - Precision Training: Use small, clearly defined targets to isolate accuracy.
  - Adaptive Control: Vary movement speed to prevent reliance on memorized pacing.
- Rule: Isolate the intended skill sufficiently that success or failure reveals meaningful information about that skill.

### 3. Hybrid Practice — Recombine Isolated Skills

- Once fundamental components are established, introduce exercises combining related techniques.
- Hybrid Skills: Abilities requiring transitions or coordination between previously isolated components.
- Examples:
  - Combining speed and precision.
  - Switching from rapid movement into sustained control.
  - Coordinating perception, timing, and execution.
- Rule: Train components independently for diagnosis, then combine them to develop flexible real-world performance.

### 4. Progressive Complexity — Add Variables One at a Time

- Establish competence in foundational skills before introducing additional demands.
- Avoid simultaneously increasing several sources of difficulty.
- Aim Training Example: Develop basic mouse control before adding character movement and strafing.
- General applications:
  - Music: Learn note patterns before adding complex rhythms or tempo demands.
  - Languages: Practice sentence construction before adding simultaneous conversation pressure.
  - Sports: Develop technique before introducing complex movement and opposition.
- Rule: Add one major variable at a time so new difficulty does not obscure fundamental technique problems.

### 5. Training vs. Assessment — Separate Practice From Testing

- Training Exercises: Designed to improve skills through repetition, variation, and feedback.
- Benchmarks: Standardized tests designed to measure current performance consistently.
- Training does not require achieving a particular score during every exercise.
- Benchmarks should not constitute the majority of practice.
- Rule: Use assessments to diagnose and measure progress; use dedicated exercises to produce improvement.

### 6. Balanced Training — Develop the Entire Skill Profile

- Construct regular sessions that cover all major skill categories.
- Include multiple repetitions of individual exercises.
- Avoid allocating all practice to the strongest or most enjoyable component.
- Voltaic Example: A roughly 65-minute fundamental routine covering tracking, clicking, and switching, with 2–5 runs per exercise.
- Rule: Maintain broad foundational training even when individual weaknesses receive additional attention.

### 7. Structured Session Order — Progress From Preparation to Demanding Work

- Begin with relatively accessible exercises that prepare the relevant systems.
- Progress into more demanding or specialized exercises.
- Organize practice into a repeatable sequence.
- Voltaic Example:
  1. Smooth or thin-target tracking.
  2. Reactive and precise tracking.
  3. Clicking.
  4. Switching.
- Qualification: Voltaic specifies this sequence but does not establish that it is universally optimal.
- Rule: Use a consistent, manageable session structure that supports preparation and deliberate practice.

### 8. Exercise Variation — Prevent Scenario Memorization

- Alternate between exercises training the same underlying ability through different conditions.
- A/B Alternation: Rotate between two variants of a training exercise.
- Prevent repeated exposure from producing familiarity with one exact scenario instead of transferable skill.
- Include occasional additional exercises to preserve engagement and adaptability.
- Rule: Repeat underlying skills while varying their specific presentation.

### 9. Benchmark Progression — Advance According to Demonstrated Competence

- Use standardized benchmarks periodically rather than constantly.
- Voltaic Guideline: Benchmark approximately weekly, averaging at least 5 attempts and preferably 10.
- Use multiple attempts to reduce the influence of unusually good or bad performances.
- Advance to harder training tiers when benchmark results demonstrate readiness.
- Treat ranks or levels as approximate indicators rather than absolute prescriptions.
- Rule: Increase training difficulty based on consistent demonstrated ability, not isolated peak performances.

### 10. Targeted Specialization — Address Diagnosed Weaknesses

- Use performance data to identify underdeveloped components.
- Add short, specialized practice blocks after establishing a foundation.
- Voltaic Example: Add 20–40 minutes of weakness-specific training for more experienced learners.
- Avoid excessive specialization before basic competence is established.
- Rule: Build broad fundamentals first; introduce targeted supplemental training when specific weaknesses become identifiable.

### 11. Focused Training Rotation — Concentrate on One Component Per Session

- VDIM-Style Training: An alternative structure emphasizing one subcategory per session.
- Combine:
  - Benchmark Exercises: Measure performance in the selected component.
  - Supporting Drills: Isolate the techniques required.
  - Harder Variants: Challenge the component beyond current comfort.
- Rotate targeted categories across training days.
- Potential benefits include deeper focused practice and spacing between sessions targeting the same skill.
- Qualification: These benefits are plausible interpretations, not confirmed explanations of the routine's design.
- Rule: Consider rotating specialized sessions when deeper component-specific practice is more useful than balanced daily coverage.

## Part 2: Underlying Learning Principles

### 1. Challenge-Skill Balance — Train Slightly Above Current Ability

- Select exercises that stretch existing competence without overwhelming it.
- Excessive Difficulty: Encourages compensatory techniques designed merely to survive or succeed at the exercise.
- Such compensations may not transfer to actual performance.
- Insufficient Difficulty: Provides too little challenge for meaningful improvement.
- If an exercise is too difficult:
  1. Reduce speed or complexity.
  2. Reestablish correct technique.
  3. Increase difficulty gradually.
- Rule: Maintain enough difficulty to stimulate improvement without forcing counterproductive technique adaptations.

### 2. Variable Isolation — Control Learning Complexity

- Introducing multiple unfamiliar demands simultaneously makes errors harder to diagnose.
- Isolate and stabilize fundamental components before combining them.
- Rule: Add complexity progressively while preserving control over the underlying technique.

### 3. Authentic Performance — Avoid Exploiting the Exercise

- Cheesing: Manipulating a task, setting, or strategy to improve its score without improving the intended skill.
- Distinguish legitimate technique improvement from benchmark-specific shortcuts.
- Optimize for transferable competence rather than artificial scores.
- Rule: Never reward performance gains that bypass the skill the exercise is supposed to develop.

### 4. Deliberate Practice — Replace Mindless Repetition

- Repetition alone does not guarantee improvement.
- Deliberate Practice Process:
  1. Observe: Record or review actual performance.
  2. Diagnose: Identify technical deficiencies.
  3. Target: Select exercises addressing those deficiencies.
  4. Execute: Practice with conscious attention to the relevant technique.
  5. Evaluate: Determine whether performance improves.
- Rule: Every focused training block should have an identifiable improvement objective.

### 5. Learning Zone vs. Performance Zone — Separate Correction From Execution

- Learning Zone:
  - Analyze mistakes.
  - Experiment with technique.
  - Identify deficiencies.
  - Deliberately modify execution.
- Performance Zone:
  - Prioritize effective execution.
  - Minimize conscious micromanagement.
  - Allow trained abilities to operate automatically.
  - Seek concentration and flow.
- Constant self-criticism during performance can disrupt automatic skills.
- Rule: Analyze and modify technique during learning; trust established technique during performance.

### 6. Consistency Over Peak Performance — Raise the Performance Floor

- A single exceptional result does not establish reliable competence.
- Track average performance and variability across sessions.
- Aim to improve:
  - Performance Floor: Results on weaker days.
  - Performance Consistency: Stability across attempts.
  - Performance Ceiling: Maximum demonstrated capability.
- Rule: Prioritize making poor performances better and reducing variability, rather than chasing occasional personal records.

### 7. Plateau Diagnosis — Inspect Fundamentals Before Adding Effort

- A Plateau is a prolonged period of limited improvement.
- Investigate foundational causes before increasing training volume:
  - Setup: Equipment and environmental conditions.
  - Posture: Physical positioning.
  - Technique: Movement and execution habits.
  - Tension: Unnecessary physical effort.
  - Practice Design: Difficulty, feedback, and exercise selection.
- Rule: When progress stalls, audit technique and training conditions before assuming additional effort is the solution.
- A plateau can also reflect fatigue, adaptation limits, or normal performance variability.

### 8. Sustainable Volume — Manage Fatigue and Diminishing Returns

- Training quality may deteriorate during excessively long sessions.
- Excessive volume increases risks of:
  - Autopilot: Repetition without deliberate attention.
  - Diminishing Returns: Progressively smaller benefits per additional minute.
  - Tension: Inefficient or strained movement.
  - Overuse Injury: Repetitive stress from excessive practice.
- Voltaic Guidelines:
  - Limit dedicated aim training to approximately 1–2 hours daily.
  - Divide training into manageable blocks with breaks.
  - Ideally spend approximately twice as much time in the actual game.
  - Dedicate 5–10 minutes daily to relaxed, low-tension aiming.
- Rule: Maximize productive practice quality rather than raw training duration.

### 9. Skill Transfer — Validate Improvement in Real Contexts

- Better performance in a training environment does not guarantee improved real-world performance.
- Transfer Problem: Isolated exercises may omit important contextual demands.
- Improvements may take time to appear in practical performance.
- Consolidation: Learning continues to stabilize after practice, so observable improvements may lag.
- Rule: Regularly practice and evaluate the complete target activity rather than relying exclusively on isolated drills.

### 10. Overload Training — Use Harder Conditions Strategically

- Overload Training: Practice selected components under conditions more demanding than ordinary performance.
- Returning to normal conditions may make the trained component feel easier.
- Aim Training Example: Practicing smooth mouse movement at higher sensitivity to challenge fine motor control.
- Overload must not encourage distorted or counterproductive mechanics.
- Rule: Introduce controlled difficulty beyond real-world demands only when the technique remains valid and improvements transfer.

## Part 3: Best Practices

*Dojo application: how this source is applied to learning design, not the source's own claim.*

### 1. Diagnose Before Prescribing

- Establish a baseline across major skill components.
- Identify weak categories rather than relying on a single overall score.
- Rule: Match exercises to diagnosed performance deficiencies.

### 2. Maintain Balanced Fundamentals

- Train every major component regularly.
- Avoid exclusively practicing preferred or already strong abilities.
- Rule: Build a broad foundation before specializing extensively.

### 3. Adapt Difficulty to Performance

- Reduce challenge when correct technique collapses.
- Increase challenge when exercises become consistently comfortable.
- Rule: Keep training near the upper boundary of reliable execution.

### 4. Use Multiple Measurements

- Evaluate performance across several attempts and sessions.
- Avoid drawing strong conclusions from one personal best.
- Rule: Use averages, trends, and consistency to determine progress.

### 5. Alternate Exercise Variants

- Rotate similar drills with different demands or presentations.
- Reduce dependence on memorized scenarios.
- Rule: Practice the same skill under varied conditions to improve generalization.

### 6. Separate Technical Work From Full Performance

- Use isolated exercises to correct specific mechanics.
- Use complete tasks to develop integrated execution.
- Rule: Alternate deliberate correction with opportunities for automatic performance.

### 7. Prioritize Relaxed Technique

- Monitor unnecessary muscular tension and inefficient movement.
- Practice maintaining control without excessive force.
- Rule: Develop efficient mechanics before attempting to compensate with speed or effort.

### 8. Record and Review Performance

- Use recordings, measurements, and benchmarks to identify mistakes that may be difficult to notice during execution.
- Compare current execution against previous sessions.
- Rule: Use observable evidence rather than intuition alone to diagnose weaknesses.

### 9. Adjust Training When Progress Stalls

- Audit technique, fatigue, difficulty, practice variety, and environmental conditions.
- Modify the specific factor limiting progress.
- Rule: Treat plateaus as diagnostic problems rather than automatically increasing training volume.

### 10. Preserve Direct Practice

- Apply isolated skills in the actual target environment.
- Monitor whether improvements translate into complete-task performance.
- Rule: Use specialized training as a supplement to real-world execution, not a replacement.

## Part 4: Common Pitfalls and Corrections

*Merged into `pitfalls.md`; kept here as this source's own list.*

| Pitfall                   | Cause                                                   | Corrective Rule                                           |
| ------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------- |
| Undifferentiated Practice | Treating a complex ability as one skill                 | Divide performance into measurable sub-skills             |
| Excessive Difficulty      | Training beyond technical competence                    | Reduce challenge and rebuild correct technique            |
| Too Many Variables        | Introducing several unfamiliar demands simultaneously   | Add complexity incrementally                              |
| Benchmark Fixation        | Prioritizing scores over skill development              | Separate assessment from training                         |
| Cheesing                  | Exploiting exercise mechanics for artificial success    | Require authentic, transferable execution                 |
| Mindless Grinding         | Repetition without diagnosis or intention               | Assign specific technical goals to practice               |
| Overfitting               | Memorizing scenarios instead of learning general skills | Alternate variants and test unfamiliar conditions         |
| Peak-Score Obsession      | Treating exceptional attempts as normal ability         | Track averages, consistency, and performance floors       |
| Persistent Plateaus       | Ignoring technique, setup, or recovery problems         | Audit fundamentals before increasing effort               |
| Overtraining              | Excessive volume, tension, or insufficient recovery     | Reduce workload and prioritize quality                    |
| Poor Transfer             | Isolated performance does not generalize                | Increase direct practice and test in realistic conditions |
| Performance Overthinking  | Conscious correction disrupts automatic execution       | Separate learning and performance zones                   |

## Core Operating Rules (this source's summary)

*Merged into `rules.md`, which is the binding list; kept here as this source's own summary.*

- Decompose Complex Skills: Identify distinct categories, sub-skills, and techniques.
- Isolate for Diagnosis: Use controlled exercises to expose specific weaknesses.
- Train Hybrids for Integration: Recombine isolated components into coordinated abilities.
- Add Variables Progressively: Establish fundamentals before increasing task complexity.
- Separate Assessment and Training: Use benchmarks to measure; use drills to improve.
- Develop Balanced Fundamentals: Maintain regular practice across all major skill categories.
- Use Structured Sessions: Organize practice into manageable sequences of increasing demands.
- Vary Exercise Conditions: Alternate similar drills to prevent scenario-specific memorization.
- Progress by Demonstrated Ability: Increase difficulty based on consistent performance rather than isolated successes.
- Target Diagnosed Weaknesses: Add specialized drills when foundational competence is established.
- Balance Breadth and Depth: Use broad routines for general development and focused sessions for specific deficiencies.
- Maintain the Challenge-Skill Balance: Train slightly beyond comfort without degrading technique.
- Avoid Artificial Success: Never optimize scores by bypassing the intended skill.
- Practice Deliberately: Observe, diagnose, target, execute, and evaluate.
- Separate Learning From Performance: Analyze mechanics during practice; allow automatic execution during performance.
- Raise the Performance Floor: Improve consistency and weaker performances alongside peak ability.
- Investigate Plateaus: Examine technique, equipment, fatigue, and training design before adding volume.
- Control Training Volume: Prioritize sustainable, attentive practice with adequate recovery.
- Reduce Unnecessary Tension: Seek efficient and relaxed execution.
- Validate Real-World Transfer: Measure whether isolated improvements strengthen complete-task performance.
- Use Strategic Overload: Introduce harder-than-normal conditions only when technique and transfer remain intact.
