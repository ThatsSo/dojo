# Dojo principles

Every Dojo mode reads this first. Other files cite principles by ID: S1–S14 for structure, A1–A7 for attitude. The tensions between principles, and how each is resolved, are in `tensions.md`. Source details and corrections are in `sources.md`.

## Thesis

**A rigorous plan, done with a Zen attitude.**

Modern learning research decides *structure*: what to practise, when, and at what difficulty. Zen decides *attitude*: how to be while practising. They answer different questions, so they fit together rather than compete. Use analytical techniques to build the plan, then do each session without attachment to immediate progress.

## The arc: explicit knowledge becoming implicit skill

This is the idea that ties the rest together. Learning moves a skill from conscious, explicit control to implicit, embodied control. Each stage has its own methods and its own right attitude, and most apparent conflicts between sources are really two stages being confused.

1. **Map (explicit).**
   - Set a specific long-term goal.
   - Break the skill into categories and subcategories, each isolating one technique, with hybrids bridging them.
   - Plan the habits to build, one at a time.
2. **Acquire (explicit, learning zone).**
   - Slow down until accuracy is near-perfect.
   - Change one variable at a time.
   - Pause and rewind on mistakes so the correction sinks in.
   - Understand before memorizing, and don't front-load theory.
3. **Automate (the transition).**
   - Retrieval and generation: test, write, teach.
   - Spacing, overlearning and repetition, until correct action becomes second nature. Repetition refines intuition.
4. **Perform (implicit, performance zone).** Flow: *a movement happening* rather than *you moving*.
   - Focus plus relaxation.
   - "Feeling" rather than reacting or predicting.
   - Notice mistakes, log them, don't judge them, keep going.
5. **Review (explicit again).**
   - Watch recordings, ask outside eyes and run rare benchmarks to find mistakes you couldn't see.
   - Feed them back into Map.

**Corollary: great players often make poor coaches.** Their knowledge has finished becoming implicit, so they can't put the earlier stages into words. A teaching system has to deliberately support the stages a master no longer remembers.

**How to use the arc:** for any feature, drill or plan, first ask *which stage is this for?* Then judge it by that stage's rules. Stopping on every mistake is right in Acquire and wrong in Perform. Conscious analysis is right in Map and Review and harmful mid-Perform.

## Structure principles

### S1. Map and decompose
**What it says:** Before practising, map the skill: why you want it, what it consists of (facts, concepts, procedures, perceptual patterns, motor techniques), and how others have learned it. Break it into categories and subcategories that each isolate one technique, with hybrid subcategories bridging the gaps, because real use blends techniques.
**Why:** *Ultralearning* (metalearning, roughly 10% of effort spent on research). Voltaic: an overall score hides *which* technique is lacking, and splitting into categories is what makes weaknesses visible ("you can't fix what you can't see"). James: "set a very specific long term goal for yourself and map out the aspects you plan on making habits one at a time."
**Signals present:** A named goal; a skill tree or category list; practice items tagged by category; reports broken down by category.
**Signals absent:** One undifferentiated "practice" mode; a single overall score; content ordered by whatever was easiest to build.
**Misapplications:** Over-splitting into dozens of categories nobody can track. Categories that don't isolate different techniques (two names for the same skill). Mapping forever instead of starting.
**Evidence:** practitioner

### S2. Directness and transfer
**What it says:** Practise as close to the real use as you can, and treat transfer from a trainer to real use as something to *check*, not assume. Substantial time belongs in the real context; drills support it, they don't replace it.
**Why:** *Ultralearning* (Directness; the transfer problem). Voltaic: trainer gains don't guarantee game gains, and you should spend at least twice as long in the game as in the trainer.
**Signals present:** Practice uses real material (real songs, real sentences, real positions). There's a path from drills to real performance, and some way to see whether gains carry over.
**Signals absent:** Drills that never meet real use; trainer scores treated as the goal; abstract exercises with no bridge to the target.
**Misapplications:** Rejecting all isolated drills as "indirect". Drills are fine when they serve a direct goal (see S3).
**Evidence:** established

### S3. All-round practice plus bottleneck drills
**What it says:** Every session covers all the categories at the learner's level. On top of that, short targeted add-ons drill the diagnosed weakest point (the rate-limiting step), then return it to whole practice. Face the uncomfortable weakness rather than avoiding it.
**Why:** Voltaic: every routine covers clicking, tracking and switching, with issue-specific add-ons. *Ultralearning*: Drill, and direct-then-drill. James: "It is in our nature to avoid the things we aren't good at… you must hammer out the kinks through repetition."
**Signals present:** A balanced default session; a way to find the weakest category; optional focused drills that feed back into whole practice.
**Signals absent:** Learners can practise only what they're already good at. Weaknesses are never surfaced. Or, at the other extreme, sessions drill only weaknesses, which kills enjoyment (A4).
**Misapplications:** Endless bottleneck grinding with no whole practice. Drills that never rejoin the full skill.
**Evidence:** practitioner

### S4. Retrieval and generation over review
**What it says:** Producing an answer from memory teaches far more than rereading it. Writing, testing and teaching are the strongest ways to encode knowledge: teach it, then quiz yourself on what you taught.
**Why:** The testing effect and the generation effect. *Ultralearning* (Retrieval). James: "writing, testing, and teaching are the best ways to absorb knowledge… It is not enough to write yourself a summary to read later." Feynman-style "explain it simply".
**Signals present:** The learner produces answers (types, plays, says, chooses) before seeing them. There are teach-back or explain-it prompts, and closed-book practice.
**Signals absent:** Read-only content; "reveal" with no attempt first; passive video or summary loops.
**Misapplications:** Recognition tasks (true/false, multiple choice) treated as recall. Testing material that was never understood (see S5).
**Evidence:** established

### S5. Understand before memorizing; no theory overload
**What it says:** Build understanding first, then use memory tools to keep it. Give explicit theory in small doses that serve the current habit. You don't need to learn everything at once.
**Why:** The SRS mindset (cards keep understanding; they don't create it). *Ultralearning* (Intuition). James: "Too much theory, or explicit knowledge, will make you feel overloaded. You do not need to learn everything at once."
**Signals present:** Concepts are introduced with examples before drilling. Theory appears at the point of need. Cards and drills come after the explanation.
**Signals absent:** Rote memorization of things the learner can't explain. Long theory dumps before any doing.
**Misapplications:** Withholding all explanation in the name of "implicit learning". Some explicit cues speed things up a lot (see S14 and `sources.md` on chicken sexing).
**Evidence:** established

### S6. Fast, informative, non-punitive feedback
**What it says:** Feedback should arrive quickly and say *what* went wrong and *how* to fix it, not just pass or fail. Mistakes you can't see yourself need review tools (recordings, replays) and outside eyes such as experts and community.
**Why:** *Ultralearning* (Feedback: outcome, informational, corrective). *Flow* (immediate feedback is a flow condition). James: "constant repetition of doing something wrong will only make you more comfortable doing things wrong… consider even checking in with experts… surround yourself with good men."
**Signals present:** Per-attempt feedback with direction (early or late, which note, which step). Replay and review of past attempts. A way to get an outside view.
**Signals absent:** Only a final score. Feedback hours later. Errors shown with no indication of the fix.
**Misapplications:** Feedback so dense it breaks flow during performance (A3, T2). Punishing tone: red flashes, buzzers, lost lives.
**Evidence:** established

### S7. Spacing for retention; repetition for automaticity
**What it says:** Spread reviews out with growing intervals so knowledge lasts. Separately, repeat skills well past first success (overlearning) until they're automatic and hold up under pressure. These are different goals with different schedules.
**Why:** The SRS mindset (the forgetting curve, the spacing effect, desirable difficulty). *Ultralearning* (Retention: spacing, proceduralization, overlearning). Woodpecker (compressed repetition for speed).
**Signals present:** Items come back over expanding intervals. Mastered items still get occasional maintenance. Core procedures are repeated beyond first success.
**Signals absent:** No review once something is "learned". Items retired forever. Cramming-style massed practice as the only mode.
**Misapplications:** Judging an interval scheme without knowing the goal. Short fixed intervals can suit exam cramming while being wrong for lifelong retention (see `audit.md`).
**Evidence:** established

### S8. Difficulty in the goldilocks zone
**What it says:** Keep the challenge just above current skill. Too easy is boring. Too hard creates anxiety *and teaches the wrong technique*: the learner reshapes their movement to survive the task and builds habits that won't carry over. When something is too hard, slow it down or simplify it rather than grinding.
**Why:** *Flow* (the challenge–skill channel). Voltaic: too-hard scenarios build habits "highly unlikely to translate", so slow them down and work on technique first. James: the "goldilocks zone", where "the speed is likely lower than you think."
**Signals present:** Difficulty adapts to performance. Manual difficulty controls exist. Content is levelled. There are tempo or timescale controls.
**Signals absent:** One fixed difficulty. Jumps between levels that are too big. No way to slow down.
**Misapplications:** Raising difficulty because the learner *can survive it* instead of because their technique holds.
**Evidence:** established

### S9. Accuracy first, then speed
**What it says:** Slow down until accuracy is near-perfect. Raise speed only once accuracy holds, and drop back when it doesn't. Doing something slowly and correctly beats doing it fast with mistakes, because repeated errors become habits.
**Why:** James: "slow down until you are nearing perfect accuracy… if you are doing one out of every ten actions incorrectly, you are creating 10% bad muscle memory" (a heuristic; see `sources.md`). Voltaic: speed up static clicking only above about 95% accuracy. Woodpecker: a careful, fully calculated first cycle before any time compression.
**Signals present:** Accuracy gates on tempo increases; tempo ladders that require clean runs; speed and accuracy both visible.
**Signals absent:** Speed or score as the only goal; tempo going up regardless of accuracy.
**Misapplications:** Demanding 100% before any speed increase (a near-perfect threshold is enough). Never pushing speed at all.
**Evidence:** practitioner

### S10. One variable at a time, within a difficulty budget
**What it says:** Change one aspect of the skill at a time until it's a habit. When one dimension goes up (speed, range, complexity), dial the others back to keep the overall challenge in the goldilocks zone, and restore them once the new dimension holds.
**Why:** James: "If you are trying to change two variables in your action at once… you will not form a habit of either… you often need to dial back some of the other variables." Voltaic: no strafe training until core mouse control is solid ("not worth adding another variable"). Piaano's spec: increase variables one at a time.
**Signals present:** Settings that raise difficulty along separate axes; progressions that introduce one new element at a time; automatic easing of other axes when one goes up.
**Signals absent:** Level-ups that change several things at once; presets that stack several new challenges together.
**Misapplications:** Freezing every other variable forever. The budget moves; it doesn't lock.
**Evidence:** practitioner

### S11. Measure separately from training
**What it says:** Benchmarks are fixed, low-noise tests taken rarely (about weekly), averaged over several runs. Training is varied and makes up most of the time. Never train on the test, and never exploit the task to raise a score ("cheesing").
**Why:** Voltaic: benchmarks are "tests or quizzes" and routines are the "lessons", and benchmark scenarios "should not be the bulk of your training". Goodhart's law. *Zen in the Art of Archery*: Herrigel's faked release was refused because it imitated the result without the skill.
**Signals present:** A separate assessment mode. Fixed test content with a set cadence. Averages and ranges rather than single best runs.
**Signals absent:** Practice and assessment are the same thing. Leaderboards on the practice task. Constant score chasing.
**Misapplications:** Benchmarking so often it becomes the training. Treating results as self-worth (A6).
**Evidence:** practitioner

### S12. The repetition-target rule
**What it says:**
- If the patterns in the practice set *are* the skill (chess motifs, chord shapes, vocabulary, code idioms), repeat the same set until recognition is instant. That's the Woodpecker approach.
- If the set stands in for a general skill (aim, sight-reading new music, reading new text), vary it so the learner doesn't memorize the material instead of building the skill.

**Why:** Woodpecker (repeat a fixed set to turn calculation into recognition; chunking). Voltaic: alternate scenarios "to avoid too much pattern recognition". Research on variable practice and transfer.
**Signals present:** Fixed sets where the patterns are the target; generated or rotating material where the skill is the target.
**Signals absent:** Sight-reading practice on the same three songs, or chord drills that never repeat a chord.
**Misapplications:** Woodpecker sets where the learner recalls answers without reproducing *why* they work (see `woodpecker.md`).
**Evidence:** practitioner

### S13. Mastery-based progression
**What it says:** Schedule the inputs, not the outcomes: fix *when* and *how much* you practise, but move on only when a habit has formed, not by a date. Don't fool yourself into thinking you're ready to move on when you aren't.
**Why:** James: "You do not, and I would say should not, need to give yourself a time frame. Give each aspect time and care until it becomes a habit." *Ultralearning*'s intensity is kept as scheduled effort (inputs). Voltaic: advance by passing the next benchmarks, and don't speed through the ranks.
**Signals present:** Unlocks and progression tied to demonstrated skill; practice reminders and schedules; no countdown pressure.
**Signals absent:** Calendar-driven curricula that advance regardless of skill; "day 30 of 30" framing as the measure.
**Misapplications:** No structure at all in the name of "no deadlines". The schedule still exists; it governs effort, not results.
**Evidence:** philosophy

### S14. Explicit vs. implicit, by how fast decisions must be made
**What it says:** Explicit knowledge suits slow, complex decisions (strategy, planning, analysis). Implicit skill suits fast execution, where conscious thought only gets in the way. Most skills need both, so teach each layer its own way. Implicit learning is helped by lots of exposure with feedback, by analogies to skills already mastered, and by *how* things are taught (engaging, familiar framing).
**Why:** James: the football example (explicit for the play call, implicit once the action starts); "great players make bad coaches"; chicken sexing. Research suggests implicitly learned skills hold up better under pressure, and that consciously monitoring a skilled movement can disrupt it (choking).
**Signals present:** Strategy or theory content is separate from execution drills. Execution drills have high volume and fast feedback. New skills are introduced through analogies to known ones.
**Signals absent:** Execution taught only through verbal rules. Learners told to think through every step at performance speed.
**Misapplications:** Treating implicit learning as "no explanation ever". One well-chosen explicit cue can speed up perceptual learning dramatically (see `sources.md`).
**Evidence:** established

## Attitude principles

### A1. Process over progress
**What it says:** Success is practising, not hitting the goal. Set goals and plans, but measure each day by whether you practised with full attention. Make goals you're in charge of rather than goals that are in charge of you. Learn to love the process, not the progress.
**Why:** *Zen in the Art of Archery* (judging by process and state of mind rather than hits). James: "Learn to love the process and not the progress… Make goals that you are in charge of rather than goals that are in charge of you." *Flow* (autotelic activity).
**Signals present:** Primary metrics count practice done (sessions, reps, minutes of focus). Goals are editable and owned by the learner. There's a way to set things aside without guilt (for example, `study.html`'s Mute).
**Signals absent:** Shaming stats; "you're behind" messaging; streaks that punish one missed day and break the habit.
**Misapplications:** Dropping measurement altogether. Progress is still tracked, as information (A6).
**Evidence:** philosophy

### A2. Discipline over motivation
**What it says:** Motivation flares and fades, so build systems that survive your moods: schedules, small default actions, routines. Discipline is the repeated decision to act without needing inspiration.
**Why:** James: "Motivation must eventually be replaced by discipline… build systems and internal standards that survive your moods. Emotional energy may start the fire, but routine keeps it lit." The SRS mindset (a daily habit that compounds).
**Signals present:** A small, obvious daily default ("today's 10 minutes"). A session that's quick to start. Routines that don't depend on novelty.
**Signals absent:** Engagement that depends on hype, rewards and dopamine mechanics. A big setup cost before every session.
**Misapplications:** Rigid schedules that ignore fatigue or injury (see A4).
**Evidence:** philosophy

### A3. Observe, don't judge
**What it says:** Feedback is information, not punishment. Notice mistakes, absorb the lesson and continue without clinging to the error. Which zone you're in decides what happens next:
- **Learning zone:** pause, rewind and correct.
- **Performance zone:** notice, log and keep going. Logged mistakes become later drills.

**Why:** James: "mistakes should be noticed, but not judged… Letting the mistake define you or your effort is itself, another mistake." *Zen in the Art of Archery*. Voltaic's separate learning and performance zones. The Stoic view of observing without judging.
**Signals present:** Neutral language and visuals. Separate drill and run-through modes. Mistakes from run-throughs collected for later practice.
**Signals absent:** Every mistake halts everything, *or* no mistake is ever revisited. Judgemental copy ("Wrong!", "Fail").
**Misapplications:** Hiding mistakes to protect feelings. Observing still means seeing clearly.
**Evidence:** philosophy

### A4. Dosed intensity
**What it says:** Rigorous, analysis-heavy mastery only works for people who still enjoy it, because the people who quit don't show up in the success stories. Dose the hard work: short, focused drill blocks, caps on volume, breaks, variety, and attention to physical strain and tension.
**Why:** James: "you will not survive such vigor if you are not still enjoying what you are doing." Voltaic: 1–2 hours at most, breaks, avoid diminishing returns, autopilot and repetitive strain; drills capped short "to not burn out". *Flow* (boredom and anxiety both end engagement).
**Signals present:** Session length guidance; short bottleneck blocks; variety within sessions; prompts to rest.
**Signals absent:** Unbounded grinding; drills that run until failure; no variety.
**Misapplications:** Using "enjoyment" as a reason to skip weaknesses (see S3).
**Evidence:** practitioner

### A5. Focus plus relaxation
**What it says:** Peak performance comes from relaxed, complete focus. Too much conscious focus creates a lag, and bracing for failure brings it on. Trying hard to relax is itself the obstacle. Breathing builds rhythm and settles the mind. Flow can only be set up (clear goals, feedback, matched challenge, no distractions), never forced. The aim is "feeling" rather than reacting or predicting.
**Why:** *Zen in the Art of Archery* (breath and an effortless release). *Flow*. James: "The answer is a mix of focus and relaxation… Making an effort to relax is the problem stopping you from relaxing." Voltaic: tension-reduction practice; aim should run near-subconsciously in games.
**Signals present:** A calm way into each session (a ritual, a warm-up). Uncluttered performance screens. Tension and breathing cues.
**Signals absent:** Noisy, distracting interfaces mid-performance; timers and alarms designed to create stress.
**Misapplications:** Treating relaxation as low effort. It's full effort without strain.
**Evidence:** practitioner (flow's conditions are established; the brain explanations for *why* relaxation helps are hypotheses, see `sources.md`)

### A6. Consistency as the measure
**What it says:** Judge progress by consistency: raise your worst day and narrow the gap between best and worst, rather than chasing peak scores. Mastery is internal, a confidence in what has been repeated and reinforced. External assessments are information, not self-worth: "You do not have to prove it endlessly; you simply have to know it."
**Why:** Voltaic: make this week's worst day better than last week's; consistency is what sets top players apart. James's measuring passage. *Flow* (intrinsic reward).
**Signals present:** Charts of averages and ranges, worst-session tracking, trend over time.
**Signals absent:** Best-score-only displays; leaderboards as the main motivator.
**Misapplications:** Ignoring measurement because "mastery is internal". Measurement is how the plan stays honest (S11).
**Evidence:** practitioner

### A7. Peace of mind
**What it says:** Peace of mind is knowing your steps add up to the right direction, even when some are small or backwards. When flow isn't available (bad environment, stress, the nature of the task), slow conscious effort still counts. Don't let the perfect be the enemy of the good; you don't always have to be optimal, but it's good to know how.
**Why:** James: the "peace of mind" passage and the closing of his section on flow. *Zen in the Art of Archery* (patience through plateaus). The *Woodpecker* plateau lesson (keep practising; breakthroughs come without warning).
**Signals present:** Long-horizon views of progress that absorb bad days. Low-energy session options ("easy day") that still count.
**Signals absent:** Every session framed as pass or fail. Bad days shown as lost progress.
**Misapplications:** Using "direction" to excuse never checking whether the direction is right (S11 and the Review stage).
**Evidence:** philosophy
