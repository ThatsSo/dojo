# Sources

Dojo's model is James's synthesis. The books and communities below are supporting evidence. Where they disagree with his synthesis, his synthesis wins, and `tensions.md` records how each disagreement is resolved. All of this is paraphrased; check claims against the evidence levels in `principles.md`.

## James's writing (primary voice)

From his essays on learning, Zen, flow, implicit vs. explicit learning, and the conflicting ideas about learning:

- **Structure plus attitude:** modern techniques decide structure, Zen decides mindset-while-doing; "a vigorous lesson plan but a Zen attitude."
- **Writing, testing and teaching** are the best ways to absorb knowledge; recall beats rereading; teach it, then quiz yourself on what you wrote.
- **The goldilocks speed:** slower than you think; slow down until you're close to perfect accuracy; accuracy over speed.
- **Mistakes are noticed, not judged.** Pause and rewind when learning; don't let a mistake break flow when performing.
- **Hidden mistakes need review:** recordings and experts ("surround yourself with good men").
- **One variable at a time:** when one goes up, dial others back; avoid theory overload.
- **No time frames:** each habit gets the time and care it needs; face your weaknesses; discomfort is where growth is.
- **Process over progress;** goals you're in charge of; discipline over motivation; systems that survive moods.
- **Zen in motion:** "a movement happening" rather than "you moving"; focus plus relaxation; "feeling" instead of reacting or predicting; breathing; trying to relax is the obstacle.
- **Peace of mind:** knowing your steps add up to the right direction, even when some go backwards.
- **Flow can only be set up, never forced.** When it isn't available, slow conscious effort still counts.
- **Implicit vs. explicit:** great players make bad coaches; tacit knowledge; chicken sexing; implicit skill holds up better under pressure; analogies and *how* you teach shape what sticks; explicit for slow decisions, implicit for fast execution.
- **Measuring isn't clinging:** mastery is internal; you don't have to prove it endlessly.

## *Ultralearning* (Scott Young, 2019)

- Self-directed, intense learning, organized around nine principles: Metalearning, Focus, Directness, Drill, Retrieval, Feedback, Retention, Intuition, Experimentation.
- **Metalearning:** spend about 10% of effort mapping why, what (concepts, facts, procedures) and how.
- **Directness:** learn in the target context; the transfer problem.
- **Drill:** find the rate-limiting step and isolate it, then go back to direct practice.
- **Retrieval and Feedback:** the testing effect; prefer corrective feedback over outcome-only feedback.
- **Retention:** spacing, proceduralization, overlearning, mnemonics.
- **Intuition:** stay with hard problems; explain things simply (Feynman).
- **Fit with James:** Dojo keeps its structure but replaces deadline-driven intensity with "schedule the inputs, not the outcomes" (T4).

## *Zen in the Art of Archery* (Eugen Herrigel, 1948)

- A long foundation of repetition before the "real" target; breathing to remove strain.
- The release should happen by itself, not be forced; the result is judged by process and state of mind rather than hits.
- Faking a technique to imitate mastery blocks real mastery.
- Patience through plateaus; learning by watching and copying the master; quieting the analytical mind.
- **Fit with James:** it supplies the attitude side (A1, A3, A5), and its anti-faking lesson supports S11 ("no cheesing").

## *Flow* (Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi, 1990)

- Optimal experience comes from absorption in a challenging, worthwhile activity.
- **Conditions:** clear goals, immediate feedback, challenge matched to skill.
- **The flow channel:** too hard leads to anxiety, too easy leads to boredom, and difficulty has to keep rising as skill grows.
- Self-consciousness disappears; activities are rewarding in themselves (autotelic); turn any task into a game; active pursuits beat passive leisure.
- **Fit with James:** it sets *optimal difficulty* on the structure side (S8) and absorption on the attitude side (A5).

## Spaced repetition and the Anki mindset

- **Research basis:** the forgetting curve (Ebbinghaus); the spacing effect; the testing effect; desirable difficulty and storage vs. retrieval strength (Bjork); interleaving.
- **Card design:** one fact per card, the minimum information principle (Woźniak).
- **The mindset:** "memory is a choice" (Nielsen); small daily effort compounds; trust the schedule; grade honestly; understand first, memorize second.
- **Fit with James:** it's the retention half of S7 and a model for A2 (discipline through a tiny daily default).

## The Woodpecker Method (Axel Smith & Hans Tikkanen, 2018)

- Built on Michael de la Maza's "Seven Circles".
- Solve a large fixed set of tactics carefully, then re-solve the whole set in about half the time, cycle after cycle.
- Each cycle turns calculation into instant recognition (chunking: de Groot; Chase & Simon). The aim is speed and automaticity, not long-term retention, so the intervals *shrink*, unlike SRS.
- Every re-solve must reproduce why the solution works, not just recall the move.
- **Fit with James:** it's the repetition half of S7 and one side of S12.

## Voltaic (FPS aim-training community)

Sources: Fundamental Routines 2.5, the Benchmarks guide, the Aim Journey guide, the FAQ, and the Season 5 benchmark announcement.

- **How aim is divided:**
  - Clicking: static, dynamic, linear.
  - Tracking: precise, reactive, control.
  - Switching: speed, evasive, stability.

  Hybrids bridge the gaps, and movement aiming is a later layer added on top.
- **Why divide it:** each subcategory isolates one technique so weaknesses become visible.
- **Routines** cover every category each session; issue-specific add-ons target weaknesses.
- **Benchmarks** (the tests) are kept apart from routines (the lessons). Benchmark weekly, averaged over 5–10 runs.
- **Difficulty:** too-hard scenarios teach habits that won't carry over, so slow them down instead.
- Alternate scenarios to avoid memorizing the scenario itself.
- **Training practice:** limit volume and take breaks; separate learning and performance zones; raise your worst day; a plateau means something fundamental is wrong.
- **Fit with James:** it adds S1's decomposition, S3, S11, and the "wrong technique" reason in S8, and it confirms T2.

## Evidence corrections

Dojo states these points accurately instead of overstating them:

1. **Flow and meditation:** describing flow as "the same state as meditation", and explaining time distortion through reduced prefrontal activity (transient hypofrontality, Dietrich), are **plausible hypotheses, not settled findings**. Flow's *conditions* (goals, feedback, matched challenge) are well supported; brain-level explanations aren't.
2. **Chicken sexing:** it's often told as "no explicit knowledge exists". But Biederman & Shiffrar (1987) found that novices given a single explicit cue identified by an expert improved sharply. Tacit skill is real, *and* well-chosen explicit cues help, which supports James's own conclusion that you often need both (T7).
3. **"10% wrong = 10% bad muscle memory"** is a useful **heuristic**, not a measured ratio. The underlying point, that repeated errors become habits, is sound.
4. **Herrigel's account** of kyūdō and Zen is a disputed interpretation (Yamada Shōji): he worked through an interpreter, and his teacher Awa Kenzō's teaching was idiosyncratic. Use it for its lessons about attitude, not as an authority on Zen.
5. **Woodpecker:** the evidence is **anecdotal** (de la Maza, Tikkanen, Smith, and players' reports). There are no controlled studies, and the success stories come from motivated players.
6. **Voltaic's VDIM** (Lowgravity56's day-by-day "Daily Improvement Method") couldn't be checked in public sources; its day split and reasoning live in Discord. Dojo uses Voltaic's documented Fundamentals and Benchmarks principles, not VDIM specifics.
